How this investment fee calculator works

This investment fee impact calculator shows how ongoing fees can reduce long‑term returns. Enter your starting balance, monthly contribution, expected gross return, fee rate, and time horizon. The tool compares growth with and without fees and estimates the “fee drag” over decades. Small percentage fees compound just like returns — the longer the horizon, the larger the gap. Use this to compare funds, platforms, or advisory fees and to understand why low costs matter for long‑term investing.

Practical tips: enter the total annual cost (fund ongoing charge + platform fee + adviser fee, each counted once); compare two realistic fee levels side‑by‑side; remember that fees reduce the balance that compounds year after year. Limitations: results assume fixed rates and steady payments/contributions. If your situation changes (rate changes, fees, irregular income), rerun the calculator with updated inputs to keep your plan accurate.

Because fees are taken every year, they lower not only your balance today but also the returns you could have earned on that money in future years.

Why investment fees matter over time

A small annual fee can look harmless, but it reduces the balance that compounds every year. Over long investing periods, that fee drag can become one of the biggest differences between two otherwise similar portfolios.

Example fee drag scenario

If two investors earn the same market return but one pays a lower total fee, the lower-cost portfolio keeps more money invested each year. That means the gap is not just the fee itself — it is also the lost growth on every fee taken out along the way.

What to compare before investing

  • Fund ongoing charges figure (OCF) or expense ratio
  • Platform or account fee
  • Advisory or management fee
  • Trading or transaction costs
  • Whether the higher fee provides real extra value

Common investment fees to watch

An investment fee calculator is most useful when you include your total annual cost, not just one headline charge. Two investments can look similar on the surface, but platform fees, fund costs, and adviser charges can create very different long-term outcomes.

Fees that often reduce long-term returns

  • Fund ongoing charge (OCF) or expense ratio: the yearly running cost of a fund or ETF, taken from the fund itself. See what the OCF includes.
  • Platform or account fee: a yearly charge for holding investments on a broker or pension platform.
  • Adviser fee: a percentage fee for portfolio advice or ongoing management.
  • Trading and dealing costs: charges each time you buy or sell, plus the transaction costs a fund pays when it trades. Neither is included in the OCF or expense ratio.

How to use this investment fee calculator well

Run the calculator with your current fee level, then test a lower-cost option. That makes it easier to see whether the extra fee is buying meaningful value or just reducing your future balance. For retirement and long-term investing, even a small annual fee difference can grow into a large cash gap over 20 to 40 years. To estimate the growth side before fees, use the Investment Calculator with your expected return and monthly contribution.

Fund charges explained: OCF, expense ratio and platform fees

Most investors pay two kinds of yearly charge: the fund’s own running costs, and the cost of the account, platform or adviser you use to hold it. They are charged separately, so the total you pay is the sum of both.

The ongoing charges figure (OCF) in the UK

UK funds publish an ongoing charges figure (OCF): the yearly cost of running the fund, shown as a percentage of your investment. It includes the fund’s annual management charge plus its other running costs (MoneyHelper: a beginner’s guide to investing). You do not get a bill for it. It is taken from the fund itself, which reduces the fund’s price.

The OCF does not include everything. The FCA notes that it leaves out transaction costs, performance fees and borrowing charges, which are shown separately in a fund’s key information document (FCA: review of cost disclosure by asset managers). New FCA rules for fund information sheets apply in full from 8 June 2027, and funds can choose to use them from 6 April 2026. Under them, a fund’s product summary shows its ongoing costs figure next to separate figures for one-off entry costs, one-off exit costs and transaction costs, plus details of any performance fee (FCA Handbook DISC 6; FCA PS25/20). Until then you may see either version, but in both the headline figure leaves those other costs out.

Expense ratio (US funds and ETFs)

In the US, the comparable figure is the expense ratio: a fund’s total annual operating expenses, such as management fees, as a percentage of its average net assets. Like the OCF, it is paid out of the fund’s assets. It does not include brokerage commissions you pay to buy or sell shares, or the transaction costs the fund pays when it trades (SEC Investor Bulletin: mutual fund and ETF fees and expenses). If you are comparing US funds, enter the expense ratio where this page says OCF.

Platform, account and adviser fees

A platform, account or administration charge pays for the service that holds your investments, such as an investment app, an ISA provider or a pension scheme. MoneyHelper lists these charges separately from a fund’s ongoing charge figure (MoneyHelper: pension scheme fees and charges). They are often a percentage of your balance each year, but some providers charge a fixed amount instead, for example £10 a year or £5 a trade. An adviser’s ongoing fee is another separate charge.

How to enter your fees without double counting

  1. Use a return before all charges in “Expected return before fees”.
  2. Add up each yearly percentage charge once: the fund’s OCF or expense ratio, plus any platform fee, plus any ongoing adviser fee. Enter the total for each option.
  3. Don’t add the OCF twice. Fund performance figures are based on the fund’s price, which already reflects charges taken from the fund. If your return assumption comes from a fund’s past performance, it is already after the OCF, so enter only your platform and adviser fees.
  4. Convert fixed fees to a percentage if you want to include them. For example, £60 a year on a £20,000 balance is 0.30% a year. Trading fees, entry and exit charges are not modelled.
  5. Transaction costs are optional. If your fund quotes a transaction costs figure, you can add it to the total to include it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an investment fee impact calculator?

It estimates how ongoing fees (like expense ratios or platform fees) can reduce your long‑term investment balance compared with a lower‑fee alternative.

Why can a 1% fee be so costly?

Fees reduce your balance every year and also reduce the future growth you would have earned on that money, so the gap can grow over decades.

Does this include taxes?

No. This tool provides educational estimates using simplified assumptions. Taxes and account type can change real outcomes.

What fees should I consider?

Common fees include the fund’s ongoing charges figure (OCF) or expense ratio, platform or account fees, ongoing adviser fees, and transaction costs, depending on your provider. Add the yearly percentage charges together and count each one once.

How can I reduce fees?

Consider low‑cost index funds or ETFs, compare platforms, and review total costs (not just one fee) before investing.

Is the OCF the same as the expense ratio?

They are the UK and US versions of the same idea: a fund’s yearly running costs as a percentage of your investment, taken from the fund itself. The exact rules for calculating them differ. UK funds call it the ongoing charges figure (OCF); US funds call it the expense ratio. Neither includes platform or adviser fees, or the fund’s trading costs. See fund charges explained.

How this calculator works

Formula

Each month: balance × (1 + r/k)^(k/12), then add the contribution where r = annual rate and k = compounding periods per year Fees: each month the balance is reduced by annual fee ÷ 12 Cost of the higher fee = value at the lower fee − value at the higher fee

Conventions

  • The cost of a fee is larger than the fees paid, because money taken in fees no longer grows.

Limitations

  • Assumes both options earn the same return before fees.
  • One-off charges, trading costs and tax are not included.
  • Fees are entered as a yearly percentage. Fixed charges (for example a flat yearly platform fee) are not modelled unless you convert them to a percentage of your balance.
Jurisdiction
Not country-specific. Enter a UK fund’s ongoing charges figure (OCF) or a US fund’s expense ratio, plus any platform and adviser fees.
Tested
Checked against independent reference calculations (closed-form formulas and published textbook values), including edge cases such as 0% rates and unreachable goals.
