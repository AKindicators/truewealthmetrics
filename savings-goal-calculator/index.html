How this savings goal calculator works

This savings goal calculator answers one of two questions. Choose How long it will take to see when you reach a target by saving a set amount each month, or How much to save each month to find the monthly amount that reaches a target by a deadline.

Enter your savings goal (the amount you want), what you have already saved, and either your monthly saving or your deadline. The interest rate is optional: it is the yearly rate your savings earn, and the calculator adds interest every month. For simple cash saving, use a low rate or 0%. For investing, use a cautious rate and remember that markets can be volatile in the short term.

Use the result as a planning target, then adjust the deadline, the goal amount or the monthly contribution until it fits your budget. Practical tips: set a deadline that matches your cash-flow reality; start with a smaller milestone if the monthly number is too high; keep goals separate (emergency fund vs holidays vs investing). If your situation changes (rate changes, fees, irregular income), rerun the calculator with updated inputs to keep your plan accurate.

Use this as a monthly savings goal calculator when you want a clear answer to “how much should I save each month?” It can also work like a savings target calculator or emergency fund calculator, because you can adjust the goal, starting balance, contribution amount and interest assumption until the plan feels realistic.

Build your budget before choosing a savings target

A savings target is easier to maintain when it fits your real monthly cash flow. If you are still learning the basics, start with this beginner budgeting guide so you can create a simple plan before deciding how much to save each month.

How much should I save each month?

Use this monthly savings goal calculator to estimate how much you should save each month to reach your target. Enter your goal, current savings, deadline and optional interest rate, then compare the result with your real budget before committing.

Without interest, the sum is simple: monthly saving = (goal − already saved) ÷ number of months until the deadline. With interest you need a little less each month, because interest does some of the work. The calculator uses the standard savings formula shown under How this calculator works.

Saving weekly instead? Multiply the monthly amount by 12 and divide by 52. For example, £375 a month is about £86.54 a week. The calculator assumes monthly deposits, so with interest, weekly saving gives a slightly different result.

If the monthly amount feels too high, try extending the deadline, lowering the target or splitting the goal into smaller milestones. A realistic plan is one you can repeat every month, not a number that fails after one unexpected bill.

Not sure what target to set? The guide How much should you save each month? covers rules of thumb, emergency funds and UK workplace pensions.

UK savings goal examples

These examples use the calculator’s own method: interest added monthly and savings paid in at the end of each month. “4% a year” here means a yearly rate with interest added every month, which works out the same as an AER of about 4.07%. If your account quotes a 4% AER instead, you would need very slightly more each month: £357.80 rather than £357.49 in example 1 (see interest rates and tax). The rates are illustrations, not current market rates. Savings rates change, so check what your own account pays.

Example 2: an emergency fund in a year

MoneyHelper suggests three to six months’ essential outgoings (MoneyHelper: how much to save for an emergency). If your essentials are £1,800 a month, three months is £5,400. Starting from nothing, reaching it in 12 months takes £450 a month at 0%, or £441.81 a month at 4% a year. Saved weekly, £450 a month is about £103.85 a week.

Example 3: £20,000 for a house deposit

With the calculator’s starting figures (£2,000 saved, £500 a month, 4% a year), you reach £20,000 in 34 months (2 years 10 months), and interest contributes £1,208.71. With no interest it takes 36 months. To reach the same goal in exactly 5 years instead, you would need £264.83 a month at 4%.

Interest rates and tax in the UK

UK savings accounts quote an AER (annual equivalent rate), which the FCA describes as how much interest the account will pay over the year, taking compounding into account (FCA: cash savings update). In the US, the equivalent is the APY (annual percentage yield), which is also based on the rate and how often interest compounds (CFPB: Regulation DD, annual percentage yield). Because this calculator adds the rate monthly, entering an AER or APY slightly overstates growth. A 4.00% AER is about 3.93% here. For short goals the difference is small.

Interest earned in a cash ISA is not usually taxed, and you can pay in up to £20,000 a year across your ISAs in 2026 to 2027 (GOV.UK: Individual Savings Accounts). Outside an ISA, most people can earn some interest tax-free. The Personal Savings Allowance is £1,000 for basic rate taxpayers, £500 for higher rate taxpayers and £0 for additional rate taxpayers, and some people also get the starting rate for savings (GOV.UK: how much savings interest is tax free). These allowances apply each tax year (6 April to 5 April), and your tax band is worked out with your savings interest added to your other income. The calculator does not model tax, so if your interest could go over your allowance, use a slightly lower rate.

Outside the UK? The calculator works the same way in GBP, USD or EUR. Only the account names and tax rules differ.

Monthly savings goal calculator for planning

A savings goal calculator is most useful when you connect it to a real decision. It turns a large financial target into one clear monthly action. This works well for an emergency fund, house deposit, holiday, wedding, car replacement or medium-term investing goal.

The key is to compare three levers: your deadline, your monthly contribution and the interest rate you assume. If you already have savings set aside, include that starting balance so the result reflects your true position. If the required monthly amount feels too high, test whether extending the deadline by 6–12 months makes the goal realistic.

  • Emergency fund: compare how long it takes to reach 3 months vs 6 months of expenses.
  • House deposit: test a stricter monthly budget against a longer timeline.
  • Holiday or wedding: use a low or zero interest rate for short-term cash savings.
  • Investing target: use cautious growth assumptions and rerun scenarios regularly.

For short-term goals, use a low or zero interest rate so the plan is conservative. For longer-term savings, compare different monthly deposits and review how optional interest changes the timeline. If your savings goal is really an investing goal, the Investment Calculator can help estimate compound growth with monthly contributions.

Emergency fund and savings target planning

You can also use this page like an emergency fund calculator. MoneyHelper suggests keeping three to six months’ essential outgoings in an instant-access account. Many people start with one month of essential expenses, then build towards three to six months depending on income stability, debt payments and family responsibilities. See example 2 for the monthly amounts.

For any savings target, add a small buffer above the exact amount you need. This helps cover price changes, fees or missed contributions without forcing you to restart the plan.

How to reach your savings goal faster

To reach a goal sooner, you need more money going in: increase your monthly contribution, reduce flexible spending, or add one-off sums such as a bonus. A better interest rate helps too, though for short goals the effect is modest: in example 1, 4% interest is worth £420.22 over two years.

Use the calculator to compare a base plan against a faster plan. For example, test your current monthly deposit, then increase it by a small amount to see how much sooner you could reach the same goal. Even small extra monthly deposits can reduce the time needed.

If the monthly amount is the problem rather than the date, a longer deadline lowers it. That will not get you there faster, but it can turn a plan you would give up on into one you can keep.

Best practice for setting a realistic savings target

Start with the monthly amount you can actually sustain, not the number you wish you could save. Then work backwards: if the target date is too soon, either increase contributions, reduce the target, or split the goal into smaller milestones. This makes the calculator more practical and helps you stay consistent instead of quitting after a few months.

A good goal savings calculator should help you choose a plan you can repeat every month. For most people, consistency matters more than setting an aggressive savings target that fails after one unexpected bill.

If your goal is tied to a broader money plan, pair this tool with the Budget Planner to find spare cash flow, and the Compound Interest Calculator if you want to compare longer-term saving or investing scenarios.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does a savings goal calculator work?

It estimates how much you need to save each month to reach a target amount by a chosen date, or how long a set monthly amount will take, based on your starting balance and interest assumption.

Should I include interest in my savings goal?

If your savings earn interest, include an estimated annual interest rate (the calculator compounds it monthly). If you leave it at 0%, it assumes savings only, which is the most conservative plan. If your bank quotes an AER or APY, enter a slightly lower figure (4.00% AER is about 3.93%).

Why does the calculator say the goal is not reached?

Your contribution may be too low for the target and horizon. With no monthly saving and no interest the goal can never be reached, and the calculator stops after 100 years. Increase monthly savings, extend the horizon, or adjust the rate.

Are contributions assumed at the start or end of the month?

We assume end-of-month contributions with monthly compounding. Real products may vary slightly.

Should I save weekly or monthly?

Monthly saving is common, but weekly can feel easier for some budgets. The best choice is the one you can stick with consistently. To turn a monthly amount into a weekly one, multiply by 12 and divide by 52.

What if I miss a month?

Missing contributions typically means you’ll need to save more later or extend your timeline. You can re-run the calculator with new inputs.

How big should my emergency fund be?

MoneyHelper suggests three to six months’ essential outgoings as a good rule of thumb, but your situation may differ based on income stability and obligations.

Is interest on my savings taxed in the UK?

Interest in a cash ISA is not usually taxed. Outside an ISA, the Personal Savings Allowance lets basic rate taxpayers earn up to £1,000 of interest each tax year without paying tax on it, and higher rate taxpayers £500; additional rate taxpayers get no allowance. Your tax band includes your savings interest, and some people with lower incomes can also use the starting rate for savings. See interest rates and tax in the UK.

How this calculator works

Formula

Required monthly saving = (goal − current × (1 + i)^n) × i ÷ ((1 + i)^n − 1) where i = annual rate ÷ 12 and n = months to the deadline (if i = 0: shortfall ÷ n)

Conventions

  • Interest is compounded monthly and deposits are made at the end of each month.
  • Time-to-goal is simulated month by month, up to 100 years.
  • Results are educational estimates and may differ from your bank’s compounding method. A quoted AER or APY already includes compounding, so the equivalent monthly-compounded rate is slightly lower.

Limitations

  • Savings rates change; the rate is assumed constant.
  • Tax on interest and account restrictions are not modelled.

Not country-specific.
